Wednesday, September 10, 2003
What they're not telling us about WHLW

My sister Naomi disappeared in October 1998. The case is officially still open. In practice, it has been closed since November of that year, when the sheriff's office stopped returning my mother's calls.

I started this blog because I needed somewhere to put what I've been finding. I'm not a journalist. I'm a graduate student in communication studies, which means I know how to research and I know how to read between the lines of official documents. I've spent the last two years quietly requesting records, cross-referencing timelines, and talking to people who knew Naomi and the station.

Here is what I know for certain: Naomi worked the overnight slot at WHLW 88.3 from May 1997 through October 1998. She was good at her job. She had regular listeners. The calls during her slot increased steadily across that period — which the station counted as a success.

Here is what I don't know: why the station's consulting psychologist, Dr. Elias Voss, was involved with the programming schedule at all. His name appears in the station's internal records as a "content consultant." I don't know what that means. I've asked the station. I've asked the county. No one has explained it to me.

I'm going to keep pulling at this. If anyone reading this has memories of the WHLW overnight broadcasts during 1997 or 1998 — what you heard, what you felt, anything unusual — I'd like to hear from you. The contact form is in the sidebar.

Friday, March 4, 2005
Conditioning patterns in the broadcast record

I've been sitting on this for a while because I wanted to be sure before I wrote it down.

I found a reference to Dr. Voss in an academic paper from 1994 — a study on audience response to repeated audio stimuli published in a small communications journal. His name appears in a footnote as a contributor to unpublished supplementary research. The language in that footnote is careful, but specific. It refers to "behavioral response patterns emerging from sustained tonal repetition in passive listening environments."

That paper was published three years before he turned up at WHLW.

What I've been piecing together from listener accounts — and I've now spoken with eleven people who called in or regularly listened to Naomi's show — is that something consistent was happening. Not to everyone. But to a subset of regular listeners. They describe: an increasing sense that the humming in the background of the broadcast meant something. A feeling that it was communicating. Some describe changing their behavior in small ways based on that feeling — routes they drove, times they stayed awake, things they said to themselves. None of them connected these experiences to the radio until I asked them directly.

I'm not saying this is what happened. I'm saying the pattern is there, and the pattern is consistent with documented conditioning methodology. Stimulus. Repetition. Reinforced response. If you designed this deliberately, this is what the data would look like.

I want to be clear: I'm not suggesting anything supernatural. I'm suggesting that someone understood how repeated audio stimuli shape behavior over time — and that this understanding was applied to Naomi's broadcasts without her knowledge or consent.

Voss is the person who fits. I haven't been able to confirm his whereabouts after 1999. His academic profile was removed from two university websites sometime between 2001 and 2003.

Saturday, February 3, 2007
The calls aren't random

I have the call log now. I don't want to say how I got it.

The incoming call record for Naomi's show — specifically the request line — shows a pattern I didn't expect. The same number appears weekly. Always after 10 PM. Always within the last two hours of her broadcast window. The duration increases each week over the final month. I don't know whose number it is. The subscriber information is sealed by court order.

I called the number. There was a voicemail. I'm not going to write down what it said here.

People don't disappear because of the sound. They disappear because they believe the sound means something.

I wrote that sentence two years ago and I stand by it. What I didn't understand then was that "believing" doesn't have to be voluntary. That's what conditioning means. You don't know you've been shaped. You just act differently, and you don't know why.

I've found references in the station records — maintenance logs, engineering memos — to after-hours archive access. A consulting badge that wasn't assigned to any named staff member. Tape duplication activity that was never logged. The maintenance engineer noted it and filed a memo. I can find no evidence anyone responded to it.

Naomi filed a complaint five days before she disappeared. I only recently found this. The complaint was never stamped as received.

I've been doing this for four years. I have more now than I've ever had. I'm going to take what I have to someone who can do something with it. If I don't post again for a while, that's why. Not because I stopped. Because I'm moving.
